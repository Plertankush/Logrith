Artificial Intelligence & ML

How machine learning and large language models work, what they are good and bad at, and how to use AI tools sensibly in real projects.

By Plert Ankush

Overview

Artificial intelligence is a broad label. In practice most of what people use today is machine learning: software that learns patterns from examples instead of following hand-written rules. Large language models, image generators and recommendation systems are all variations on that idea, trained on very different data for very different jobs.

Understanding the basics changes how you use these systems. A language model predicts likely text; it does not look facts up unless it is connected to a source. That single idea explains why it can write fluent prose, why it sometimes states false things confidently and why good prompts and good checks matter.

What this section covers

  • How language models are trained and why they produce errors
  • Tokens, context windows and what they mean for long documents and cost

  • Privacy and data handling when you send text to an AI service

Core concepts

Token

The small chunk of text a model reads and writes. Usage limits and pricing are usually counted in tokens, not words.

Context window

The amount of text a model can consider at once, including your prompt and its own reply.

Hallucination

A fluent but unsupported answer. It is a property of how the model generates text, so checking matters for facts, numbers and citations.

Fine-tuning vs prompting

Prompting changes the instructions you give a model. Fine-tuning changes the model itself and needs examples and effort. Most projects should start with prompting.

Where to start

Start with a small, low-risk task you already do by hand, such as summarising meeting notes or drafting a first outline. Compare the AI result with your own, note what it gets wrong and decide what a reviewer must always check. That habit scales better than any single prompt trick.
