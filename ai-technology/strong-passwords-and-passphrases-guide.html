Strong Passwords and Passphrases: What Actually Works

By Plert Ankush · Published Nov 12, 2026 · Updated Nov 12, 2026 · 4 min read

Most advice about passwords is outdated, confusing or both. People are told to add symbols, change them every ninety days and memorise a different one for every site, which is not possible for a human. The result is predictable: reused passwords and small variations of the same word.

Modern guidance, including the digital identity guidelines published by NIST, has moved toward a simpler idea: make passwords long and unique, use a password manager, and add a second factor. This guide shows how.

Key takeaways

  • Length and uniqueness matter more than clever substitutions.
  • A passphrase of several random words is strong and easier to remember.
  • Use a password manager so every account has its own unique password.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication, starting with your email.

How passwords actually get stolen

Understanding the real threats explains the advice.

  • Credential stuffing. Attackers take email and password pairs leaked from one site and try them on others. If you reuse a password, one breach exposes many accounts. This is the most common problem for ordinary users.
  • Phishing. A convincing fake login page captures whatever you type, however strong the password.
  • Guessing and cracking. Attackers test common passwords and, if they obtain a database of hashed passwords, try enormous numbers of guesses offline.
  • Malware. Software on your device can record keystrokes or steal saved data.

Notice that a "complex" eight-character password such as P@ssw0rd! helps against none of the first, second or fourth threats, and little against the third because attackers know these patterns.

Why length beats complexity

The strength of a password is its unpredictability, often measured in bits of entropy. Each extra random character multiplies the number of possibilities an attacker must try. Adding length scales the difficulty far more than swapping an a for an @, because humans substitute in predictable ways.

NIST's guidelines (SP 800-63B) recommend allowing long passwords, discourage forced composition rules such as "must include a symbol" and discourage mandatory periodic changes unless there is evidence of compromise. Recent revisions also set higher minimum lengths where a password is the only authentication factor. Check the current text for exact numbers if you are setting policy.

To see how a password scores, try the Password Strength checker. It runs in your browser, but as a habit, test patterns rather than your real passwords.

Passphrases: strong and memorable

A passphrase is a password made of several random words, such as correct-horse-battery-staple, the famous example from a well-known comic about password strength. If the words are chosen randomly from a large list, the result is both strong and easier to remember than a jumble of symbols.

The maths is simple. A word list of 7,776 words, as used by the Diceware method, gives about 12.9 bits of entropy per word. Six words give roughly 77 bits, which is far beyond practical guessing. The key word is random: a favourite quote or a song lyric is much weaker than words picked by dice or a generator.

Use the Passphrase Generator to create one, and the Password Generator for long random strings when a site needs them.

Use a password manager

You cannot remember dozens of unique long passwords, and you should not try. A password manager stores them in an encrypted vault protected by one strong master passphrase, fills them in on the right sites and generates new ones.

  1. Choose a reputable manager. Built-in options in browsers and operating systems are better than reusing passwords, and dedicated managers add features such as sharing and breach alerts.
  2. Create a long, random master passphrase and memorise it. Write it down once and keep the paper somewhere safe at home if you need a backup.
  3. Start with your most important accounts: email, banking, cloud storage and anything that can reset other accounts.
  4. Let the manager generate a unique password for each site and replace old ones as you log in.

A manager that fills credentials only on the correct web address also helps against phishing, because it will not fill a fake domain.

Add a second factor

Two-factor authentication (2FA) requires something beyond the password, so a stolen password alone is not enough. Not all second factors are equal:

MethodProtectionNotes
SMS codesBasicBetter than nothing, but vulnerable to SIM-swap attacks and phishing.
Authenticator app codesGoodCodes generated on your device. Can still be phished in real time.
Passkeys and security keysStrongestPhishing-resistant because they are tied to the real website.

Prioritise your primary email account, because password resets for everything else usually go there.

A practical checklist

  • Unique password for every account, generated and stored by a manager.
  • A long random passphrase as the master password.
  • 2FA on email, financial accounts and anything important, using an app or passkey where offered.
  • Check whether your email appears in known breaches using a trusted service such as Have I Been Pwned, then change affected passwords.
  • Be suspicious of urgent messages asking you to log in. Navigate to the site yourself instead of clicking links.
  • Keep devices and apps updated.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a password be?

Longer is better. For passwords you must memorise, use a passphrase of at least five or six random words. For everything else, let a manager generate a long random string, commonly 16 characters or more.

Should I change my passwords regularly?

Current guidance generally advises against forced periodic changes. Change a password when there is a sign it has been exposed, such as a breach notification.

Are password managers safe?

A well-designed manager encrypts your vault with your master password, and using one is widely considered safer than reusing passwords. Protect it with a strong master passphrase and 2FA.

Is it safe to test my password on an online checker?

Prefer checkers that run entirely in your browser, and avoid typing your real passwords into any tool you do not trust. Testing a similar pattern is safer.
