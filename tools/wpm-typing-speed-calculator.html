Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good typing speed?

The average typing speed is around 40 WPM. Professional typists often reach 65-75 WPM, while touch-typing experts can exceed 100 WPM.

What's the difference between gross and net WPM?

Gross WPM counts all words typed without penalty. Net WPM subtracts errors from the total, giving a more accurate picture of effective typing speed.

How is a "word" defined for typing speed?

By convention, one "word" equals 5 characters (including spaces), regardless of actual word length, providing a standardized way to compare typing speeds.

Does the live test measure reading speed too?

This tool measures typing speed specifically. Reading speed is a related but different skill, typically measured separately.

Is anything I type uploaded to a server?

Why does my WPM seem to change throughout the test?

How WPM Is Calculated

Words per minute (WPM) is calculated by dividing the number of words typed by the time taken in minutes. Since actual word lengths vary widely, the typing industry standard defines one "word" as exactly 5 characters (including spaces) — this way, a burst of typing gets measured consistently regardless of whether the actual words were short or long. Gross WPM is this raw calculation; net WPM subtracts a penalty for errors, giving a more realistic measure of productive typing speed.

Gross WPM vs. Net WPM vs. Accuracy

  • Gross WPM — total words typed divided by time, without accounting for mistakes.
  • Net WPM — gross WPM minus errors per minute, reflecting your effective, usable typing speed.
  • Accuracy — the percentage of typed words that were correct, a separate but related measure of typing quality.

How Typing Speed Compares Across Skill Levels

Typing speed varies enormously depending on experience, technique, and practice. Hunt-and-peck typists, who look at the keyboard and use only a few fingers, commonly type in the 10-25 WPM range. Casual touch-typists who learned proper finger placement but don't type daily for work often land somewhere in the 35-50 WPM range. Professionals who type extensively — data entry specialists, transcriptionists, programmers — frequently reach 65-85 WPM with practice, and competitive typists and stenographers can exceed 120 WPM, though these top speeds require years of dedicated practice and often specialized keyboard techniques.

Why Accuracy Matters as Much as Raw Speed

Raw typing speed alone can be a misleading measure of real-world typing ability. Someone typing 80 gross WPM with 70% accuracy is spending significant time going back to fix errors, which often makes their effective output slower than someone typing a more modest 55 WPM at 98% accuracy with minimal corrections needed. This is exactly why net WPM — which factors in errors — is generally considered the more meaningful number for comparing typing proficiency, rather than gross WPM alone.

Why Typing Speed Matters

  • Job requirements — many administrative, data entry, and transcription roles specify a minimum WPM requirement.
  • Productivity — faster, accurate typing directly reduces time spent on any text-heavy work.
  • Skill tracking — measuring progress as you practice and improve your typing.
  • Remote work and coding — roles involving heavy daily typing benefit measurably from even modest speed improvements over time.

Tips for Improving Your Typing Speed

Consistent, deliberate practice tends to improve typing speed far more reliably than simply typing a lot in daily work, since everyday typing often reinforces existing habits rather than correcting them. Focusing on accuracy first, and allowing speed to increase naturally as muscle memory develops, tends to produce better long-term results than prioritizing raw speed at the cost of frequent errors. Proper touch-typing technique — keeping fingers on the home row and avoiding looking at the keyboard — is the single biggest factor separating faster typists from slower ones.

Setting Realistic Improvement Goals

Typing speed tends to improve in a predictable pattern: rapid early gains as basic finger placement and habits form, followed by a slower, more gradual climb as further improvement depends increasingly on consistent practice rather than simply learning the basics. Someone starting from hunt-and-peck typing can often reach a comfortable 40-50 WPM within a few weeks of regular practice, while pushing from 50 to 80 WPM typically takes considerably longer and benefits from deliberate accuracy-focused practice rather than just typing more in daily work. Setting an incremental target — a few WPM higher than your current baseline — tends to feel more achievable and motivating than aiming directly for an ambitious long-term number.
