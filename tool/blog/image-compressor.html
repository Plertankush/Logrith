Image Compressor: The Complete Guide to Shrinking Photos Without Losing Quality

Every image you upload to the web costs someone time — a slower page load, a stalled form submission, a bloated email attachment. Compression fixes that, but only if you understand what you're actually trading away. This guide covers how image compression works, how to use Logrith's six-mode compressor effectively, and how it stacks up against TinyPNG and Squoosh, the two tools most people reach for first.

Compress an Image

What Image Compression Actually Does

Every digital image is, underneath its visual appearance, a grid of pixel values that needs to be stored as bytes on disk. Compression is the process of representing that same grid using fewer bytes — either by removing redundancy that doesn't change how the image looks (lossless compression), or by selectively discarding detail the human eye is least likely to notice (lossy compression). Both approaches produce a smaller file; they differ in whether the original pixel data can be perfectly reconstructed afterward.

PNG is a lossless format by design — decompressing a PNG always gives you back the exact original pixels. This makes PNG ideal for screenshots, logos, and graphics with sharp edges or transparency, but it means PNG file sizes for photographs stay relatively large, since there's genuinely a lot of unique pixel-level detail to preserve. JPEG, by contrast, is inherently lossy: it uses a mathematical transform that groups similar colors and discards fine detail in ways that are calibrated to be hard for the eye to notice, which is why JPEGs of photographs can be five to ten times smaller than an equivalent PNG at a similar visual quality.

This is the first decision point in any compression workflow, and it's one a lot of people skip past without realizing it: the format you're compressing already determines a huge amount of your ceiling for savings, before you've touched a single quality slider.

Why Image File Size Matters More Than It Seems

It's easy to treat file size as a minor technical detail, but it compounds in ways that are easy to underestimate. A single 4MB photo uploaded to a web page adds measurable load time on a mobile connection — and most websites don't have just one image, they have dozens. Google's Core Web Vitals, which factor directly into search ranking, are heavily influenced by how quickly the largest visible image on a page finishes loading. An unnecessarily large hero image can single-handedly push a page from a "good" to a "needs improvement" score.

Beyond web performance, file size limits show up constantly in ordinary tasks that have nothing to do with websites: email attachment caps, government and exam portal upload limits (often as strict as 20-50KB for ID photos), messaging apps that auto-recompress large images in ways you don't control, and cloud storage quotas that fill up faster than expected. Understanding compression isn't just a web developer's concern — it's a genuinely useful everyday skill.

The Six Compression Modes, Explained

Most image compressors online give you exactly one lever: a quality slider. That's fine when quality reduction is actually the right tool for the job, but it's the wrong tool more often than people realize. Logrith's Image Compressor exposes six distinct modes because the "right" way to shrink an image depends entirely on what you're trying to accomplish.

1. Quality Mode

This is the classic approach: pick a quality percentage, and the tool re-encodes the image at that quality level, primarily affecting how aggressively JPEG-style compression discards fine detail. This is the right choice when your image's pixel dimensions are already appropriate for its use, and you just need a smaller file — a typical camera photo being prepared for a blog post, for example.

2. Resize Mode

Sometimes the real problem isn't compression quality at all — it's that the image is simply bigger than it needs to be. A photo straight from a modern phone camera can be 4,000 pixels wide, when the webpage displaying it is only 800 pixels wide. Resizing down to the actual display dimensions, before any quality reduction, often produces a far smaller file with visibly better quality than aggressively compressing an oversized original.

3. Exact Dimensions Mode

Related to resizing but more specific: sometimes you need an image to be precisely a certain width and height, not just proportionally scaled down — a profile picture requiring exactly 400×400px, for instance. This mode handles that directly.

4. Target File Size (KB) Mode

This is the mode most people don't realize they need until they hit a wall: a form that says "photo must be under 50KB" with no further guidance. Rather than guessing at a quality percentage and checking the result repeatedly, target-size mode automates that search — trying multiple quality levels until it finds one that lands close to your specified size, saving the tedious manual trial-and-error.

5. Preset Mode

For common destinations — web use, email attachments, WhatsApp sharing — there's usually a well-understood "good enough" balance of size and quality that doesn't require thinking through the tradeoffs yourself each time. Preset mode packages these as one-click options.

6. Format Conversion Mode

Sometimes the biggest single win available isn't adjusting quality at all — it's switching formats. A PNG screenshot of a photograph converted to JPEG, or an old JPEG converted to WebP, can shrink dramatically simply because the target format is inherently better suited to that kind of image content.

Lossy vs. Lossless: Which Should You Choose?

If your image is a screenshot, a logo, a diagram, or anything with sharp edges, flat colors, or text — stick with a lossless approach or very high quality settings (85%+), since lossy compression tends to introduce visible artifacts around sharp edges that weren't originally photographic. If your image is a photograph with natural gradients, lighting, and texture, moderate lossy compression (65-85% quality) is usually the better choice, since photographs compress far more efficiently this way with minimal visible impact, and the resulting file size difference is substantial.

It's worth understanding why this split exists at a slightly deeper level. Lossy JPEG-style compression works by transforming blocks of pixels into a frequency representation and then discarding the high-frequency components — the parts of the image that represent very fine, rapid changes in color or brightness. Natural photographs are full of gradual transitions (skin tones, sky gradients, foliage texture) where discarding fine high-frequency detail is genuinely hard to notice, because the eye is naturally forgiving of small variations in continuous tone. A sharp black line of text on a white background, by contrast, is almost entirely high-frequency information — the transition from black to white happens instantly, with no gradual blend — which is exactly the kind of content this compression method handles poorly, producing visible "ringing" or blurring artifacts around the edge.

This is also why a scanned document saved as JPEG often looks noticeably worse than the same document saved as PNG, even at a fairly high JPEG quality setting: the sharp text edges are precisely the content JPEG compression is worst at preserving. If you're compressing anything with text, line art, or hard edges, either stay lossless or push the quality setting high enough that the artifacts become genuinely invisible — usually 90% or above for this kind of content.

Understanding the File Formats Behind the Numbers

Choosing the right output format is often a bigger lever than any quality slider. Here's what actually distinguishes the common formats you'll encounter:

  • JPEG — the long-standing default for photographs. Lossy by design, broad support everywhere, no transparency support. Still the safest universal choice when compatibility matters more than squeezing out the last few percent of file size.
  • PNG — lossless, supports transparency, ideal for screenshots, logos, and graphics. Poor choice for photographs specifically because lossless compression can't exploit the redundancy that lossy methods can, leaving photographic PNGs much larger than they need to be.
  • WebP — a more modern format supporting both lossy and lossless modes, transparency, and typically 25-35% smaller file sizes than an equivalent-quality JPEG. Supported by all current browsers, making it a strong default for web images today.
  • AVIF — the newest widely-adopted format, often producing the smallest files of all at a given visual quality, sometimes beating WebP by another 20-30%. Support is now solid in modern browsers, though encoding is more computationally intensive.

The practical takeaway: if your priority is maximum compatibility (email attachments, older systems, print workflows), JPEG or PNG remain the safe defaults. If your priority is web performance and your audience is on modern browsers, converting to WebP is close to a free size reduction with no meaningful compatibility downside in 2026.

How Browser-Based Compression Actually Works

It's worth understanding what's actually happening when a tool like this one says it compresses "entirely in your browser." Modern browsers expose a Canvas API that lets JavaScript draw an image onto an invisible in-memory canvas, then export that canvas back out as a new image file at a specified quality level and format. This is a genuine, native browser capability — not a simulation or a lightweight approximation — which means the compression quality achievable this way is comparable to what a desktop image editor would produce using the same underlying codecs.

The practical implication is significant for privacy: at no point does your image data need to travel over a network connection. The entire read-modify-export cycle happens within your browser's own memory space, using code that runs locally. This is fundamentally different from how server-based tools like TinyPNG work, where your file is transmitted to a remote server, processed there, and the result sent back — a process that's usually fast and generally trustworthy for reputable services, but which inherently means a third party's infrastructure has, at minimum, briefly handled your original file.

For most everyday images, this distinction won't matter much. But for anything involving personal documents, medical images, legal paperwork, unreleased creative work, or business-confidential material, working with a tool that genuinely never uploads your file is a meaningfully different privacy posture, not just a marketing claim.

Real-World Scenarios: Which Mode to Use

Understanding the six modes conceptually is one thing — knowing which one to reach for in a specific situation is more useful. Here are common real-world scenarios matched to the mode that solves them most directly:

  • "My blog is loading slowly because of image sizes." Start with Resize mode to bring oversized camera photos down to your actual display width, then apply Quality mode at 70-80% on top. This combination typically produces the largest realistic size reduction for web content.
  • "This government exam portal requires a photo under 20KB." Use Target File Size mode directly. Manually guessing quality percentages to hit a hard size requirement wastes far more time than letting the tool search for the right setting automatically.
  • "I need a perfectly square 500×500 profile picture." Use Exact Dimensions mode, which handles both the resizing and any necessary cropping to hit precise pixel dimensions rather than just scaling proportionally.
  • "I just need to email this photo without thinking too hard about it." Use Preset mode and select the email preset, which applies a sensible pre-calibrated size and quality balance without requiring you to understand the tradeoffs yourself.
  • "This PNG screenshot of a photo is huge for no reason." Use Format Conversion mode to convert it to JPEG or WebP — this alone often shrinks photographic PNGs by 70% or more before you've even touched a quality setting.

Common Mistakes That Waste Compression Potential

  • Compressing before resizing. If an image's pixel dimensions are much larger than needed, compress after resizing, not instead of it. A correctly-sized image at high quality often beats an oversized image at low quality, both in file size and visual result.
  • Using PNG for photographs. PNG's lossless nature is wasted on photographic content, which has too much genuine per-pixel variation to compress well losslessly. Converting a photographic PNG to JPEG or WebP is often the single biggest size reduction available.
  • Compressing already-compressed images repeatedly. Each round of lossy re-compression can compound quality loss, since each pass discards additional detail based on an already-degraded source. Keep an original, uncompressed master file and compress from that each time you need a new output, rather than compressing an already-compressed file again and again.
  • Ignoring target requirements. If a form specifies "under 50KB," guessing at a quality percentage wastes time. Use target-size mode directly instead of iterating manually through trial and error.
  • Applying the same settings to every image type. A setting that works well for a photograph (moderate lossy compression) can look noticeably worse applied to a screenshot or graphic with text. Match your approach to the actual content, not a one-size-fits-all default.
  • Not checking the result before using it. Especially at aggressive compression levels, always preview the compressed output before relying on it for something important, like a profile photo or a print-bound image.

Compression for Specific Use Cases

Web and Blog Images

For web use, the general target is images that load fast without visibly compromising the page's design. A practical rule of thumb: resize to no more than your image's actual maximum display width (accounting for high-DPI screens, roughly 1.5-2x the CSS display width), then compress at 70-80% quality for photographs, or convert to WebP for an additional reduction with no quality tradeoff at all.

Government and Institutional Forms

These typically specify both a maximum file size and sometimes exact pixel dimensions. Use Exact Dimensions mode first if dimensions are specified, then Target File Size mode to hit the size requirement — checking the result against both constraints before submitting, since some portals reject files that don't meet both criteria simultaneously.

Email Attachments

Most email providers cap total attachment size well below what a handful of full-resolution photos would require. Preset mode's email option is calibrated for this — typically landing images in a size range that keeps a handful of photos comfortably under common provider limits, without needing to calculate anything yourself.

Print and Archival Use

If an image needs to be printed or archived at high fidelity, avoid aggressive lossy compression entirely. Use high quality settings (90%+) or stick with lossless PNG, since print output reveals compression artifacts far more readily than a screen does, particularly at larger physical sizes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does compressing an image reduce its quality?

At moderate quality settings (roughly 65-85%), the visual difference is imperceptible to most people at normal viewing size. Quality loss only becomes clearly visible at very aggressive compression, typically below 40%.

Is it safe to compress images with sensitive content online?

It depends entirely on whether the tool processes images in your browser or uploads them to a server. Logrith's Image Compressor runs entirely client-side using the Canvas API, so images never leave your device at any point.

What's the difference between lossy and lossless compression?

Lossless compression shrinks a file without discarding any image data, typically saving 10-30%. Lossy compression discards some detail the eye notices least, achieving much larger reductions (50-80%) at a controllable quality tradeoff.

Should I resize or compress first?

Resize first if your image's pixel dimensions are larger than what you'll actually display or need. Resizing to the right dimensions before compressing typically gives a much better result than compressing an oversized image alone.
