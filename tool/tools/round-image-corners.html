Frequently Asked Questions

How do I round the corners of an image?

Upload the image, set a radius with the slider or by typing a number, check the live preview and click Download. The corners outside the curve become transparent in PNG and WEBP.

Can I use different radii for each corner?

Yes. Untick "Same radius for all corners" and enter a separate value for top left, top right, bottom right and bottom left. This is useful for speech-bubble shapes, tabs and cards that connect to other elements.

What is the difference between pixels and percent?

Pixels set the radius in absolute units of the image. Percent is relative to the shorter side of the image, so 50% always produces a fully rounded end, a circle on a square image and a pill on a wide one. Use percent when you want the same look on images of different sizes.

Will the corners be transparent?

Yes, in PNG and WEBP. JPG does not support transparency, so for JPG the tool fills the corners with the background color you select, white by default.

Does rounding the corners reduce image quality?

No resampling takes place. The original pixels are drawn at full size and clipped by the rounded shape, so the visible area is unchanged. Saving as JPG or WEBP applies normal compression, while PNG is lossless.

What happens if the radius is larger than the image allows?

The tool limits the radius to half of the shorter side, which is the largest curve that fits. If two neighbouring corners would overlap, their radii are scaled down together, the same way CSS border-radius behaves.

Is my image uploaded anywhere?

No. The image is processed on a canvas in your own browser and the download is created locally.

About the Round Image Corners Tool

The Logrith Round Image Corners tool softens the corners of any picture with a radius you control. Set one value for every corner or a different value for each, watch the result update live, and download a PNG or WEBP with transparent corners. It works entirely in your browser, with no upload and no watermark.

Rounded corners are one of the most common visual details in modern design. App icons, cards, buttons, screenshots in documentation and product shots all look friendlier and more finished with a gentle curve. Websites can round images with the CSS border-radius property, but that only works where you control the page. When you need the rounded shape inside the image file itself, for a presentation, a social post, a document or a marketplace listing, you need to bake it into the pixels. That is what this tool does.

Pixels or percent?

You can set the radius in two units. A pixel radius is absolute: 40px means a curve with a 40-pixel radius no matter how large the picture is, so the same number looks very different on a small thumbnail and a large photo. A percentage radius is relative to the shorter side of the image, so the same percentage gives a similar look at any size. A value of 50% is the maximum and produces a fully rounded end: a circle on a square image and a pill shape on a wide one.

Use pixels when you are matching a design that specifies a radius, for example a 12px card style. Use percent when you apply the same style to many images of different sizes.

Same radius or each corner separately

By default all four corners share one radius. Unlink them to set top left, top right, bottom right and bottom left independently. Uneven corners are handy for chat-bubble shapes, tab-style headers that round only the top, cards that join onto a flat edge, and decorative layouts where one corner is left sharp. Setting a corner to zero keeps it perfectly square.

What the presets do

  • Square removes rounding completely.
  • Soft applies a light curve, about 15% of the largest possible radius, similar to a subtle card style.
  • Rounded is a clear, friendly curve, about 30% of the maximum.
  • App icon is a generous curve, about 60% of the maximum, similar to mobile app icons.
  • Pill / circle uses the full radius, so the shorter sides become completely round.

How the rounding is drawn

The browser draws a rounded-rectangle path the size of the image, clips to it, and paints the original picture inside. Pixels outside the path are not drawn, which leaves them transparent. If the radii of two neighbouring corners add up to more than the side they share, they are scaled down together so the curves never overlap, matching how CSS treats border-radius. Nothing is resampled, so the visible part of the picture keeps its original sharpness.

Choosing a format

PNG is lossless and keeps transparency, so it is the safest choice for logos, screenshots and interface graphics. WEBP also keeps transparency and is usually noticeably smaller, which is helpful for websites. JPG produces the smallest files for photographs but has no transparency, so the corners are filled with a background color. Choose a color that matches the page the image will sit on, or the corners will look like a visible box.

Common use cases

  • App and software screenshots in documentation, tutorials and store listings.
  • Product and listing photos that need a softer, consistent look across a shop.
  • Social media and blog images where a rounded card style matches your brand.
  • Presentation slides and documents that cannot round pictures on their own.
  • Icons, avatars and badges, using the pill preset or a high percentage for round ends.

Tips for good-looking rounded images

Match the radius to the rest of your design. If your buttons and cards use a 12px radius, use roughly the same on your images, scaled to the image size. Larger images can take a larger radius before it looks heavy. Keep important content, such as text in a screenshot, away from the very corners, because the curve removes a small triangle of pixels from each one. If your image already has a visible border, round it by the same amount as the border so the line follows the curve.

Rounded corners vs a circle crop

Rounded corners keep the whole picture and only trim the corners. A circle crop cuts the picture down to a round shape and discards the rest.
