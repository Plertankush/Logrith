How to Write a Clear Value Proposition (With a Simple Template)

By Plert Ankush · Published Nov 12, 2026 · Updated Nov 12, 2026 · 3 min read

A visitor lands on your website and decides within seconds whether it is for them. If they cannot tell what you offer, who it is for and why it is better than the alternatives, they leave. A value proposition is the short statement that answers those questions, and it is the foundation of your homepage, your pitch and your branding.

This guide shows how to write one, with a template, examples and a quick test to check that it works.

Key takeaways

  • A value proposition states who you serve, what outcome you deliver and why you are the better choice.
  • Customer outcomes matter more than feature lists.
  • Use the words your customers use, not internal jargon.
  • Test it: can a stranger explain it back after five seconds?

What a value proposition is

A value proposition is a clear statement of the benefit your product delivers and why a specific customer should choose it over alternatives. It is not a slogan and not a feature list. It is the promise that makes a visitor think "that is for me".

It differs from positioning, the place your product holds in a customer's mind relative to other options. Positioning is the strategy and the value proposition is how you express it to customers.

The four ingredients

  1. Customer: who it is for, as specifically as you can.
  2. Problem or need: what they are trying to get done.
  3. Outcome: the result they get, ideally measurable or concrete.
  4. Difference: why this is better than what they use today.

A simple template

Start with this sentence and fill the blanks:

We help [customer] do [job or outcome] so they can [benefit], without [main pain or cost].

Example: "We help freelance designers send invoices and get paid on time, so they can spend less time chasing clients, without learning accounting software."

A more formal version is the positioning statement popularised by Geoffrey Moore in Crossing the Chasm:

For [target customer] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], our product [difference].

Use it as an internal working document. Then turn it into natural language for your homepage.

Lead with outcomes, not features

Features describe what a product has. Benefits describe what the customer gets. Customers buy outcomes.

Feature-focusedOutcome-focused
AI-powered scheduling engineBook meetings without the back-and-forth emails
256-bit encrypted storageKeep client files private, even if a device is lost
Browser-based image compressionShrink images in seconds without uploading them anywhere

Keep the feature as proof, placed after the benefit.

Use your customers' words

Your own product vocabulary rarely matches your customers'. Go back to your customer interviews, support emails and reviews, and copy the phrases people use to describe their problem. Those phrases will resonate more than clever language, and they are often the words people type into search engines, which helps SEO too. Our guide to customer discovery interviews explains how to collect them.

Where to use it

  • Homepage headline and subheading. The headline states the outcome, and the subheading adds who it is for and how.
  • Page titles and meta descriptions.
  • Social profiles, bios and your email signature.
  • Pitch decks, sales calls and onboarding emails.

Consistency builds recognition. When the same promise appears everywhere, people remember it. A character counter helps you fit it into titles, descriptions and bios.

The five-second test

  1. Show your homepage to someone who matches your customer for five seconds, then hide it.
  2. Ask: what does this company do, who is it for and what would you do next?
  3. If they cannot answer, simplify your wording and test with new people.

Five or six people is enough to spot a pattern. If most struggle in the same way, the message needs work.

Common mistakes

  • Being vague: "The all-in-one platform for modern teams" could be anything.
  • Using jargon: words customers do not use or understand.
  • Trying to speak to everyone, which makes the message weak for each group.
  • Listing features with no outcome.
  • Making claims you cannot prove, such as "the best" or "number one" without evidence.

From message to brand

Once the message is clear, the visual identity supports it: a consistent set of colours, fonts and a simple logo used the same way everywhere. Tools like a colour converter and a palette generator help you pick and document colours. Keep it simple. Consistency matters more than complexity.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a value proposition be?

One or two sentences. If it takes a paragraph, it is probably unfocused.

What is the difference between a value proposition and a tagline?

A tagline is a short, memorable phrase for branding. A value proposition explains the specific benefit and for whom, and may be longer and more concrete.

Should I have a different value proposition for each customer type?

If your customer groups have different needs, yes, you may need separate messages or landing pages for each. Start with the group you serve best.

How do I know if my value proposition works?

Run the five-second test with people who match your customer, and watch whether your page converts visitors to sign-ups or enquiries after changing the wording.
