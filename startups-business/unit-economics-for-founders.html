Unit Economics for Founders: Margins, Break-Even and Cash Flow

By Plert Ankush · Published Nov 12, 2026 · Updated Nov 12, 2026 · 3 min read

A business can have happy customers and rising revenue and still fail, because the numbers behind each sale do not work. Unit economics is the discipline of looking at the money in and out for a single customer or order and asking whether growth makes you healthier or poorer.

This guide explains the key terms with simple worked examples. The numbers are illustrative, so replace them with your own.

Key takeaways

  • Gross margin shows what is left after the direct cost of delivering what you sell.
  • Compare customer lifetime value with the cost of acquiring a customer.
  • Break-even tells you the volume you need to cover fixed costs.
  • Profit and cash are different. Plan your runway around cash.

Revenue, costs and margin

Revenue is what customers pay you. Cost of goods sold (COGS), or cost of revenue for software, is the direct cost of delivering the product: hosting, payment fees, materials, shipping and customer support tied to usage.

Gross margin = (Revenue minus COGS) divided by Revenue.

Example: You sell a subscription for $30 a month. Hosting, payment fees and support cost $6 per customer. Gross profit is $24, so gross margin is 24 ÷ 30 = 80%.

A percentage calculator makes this quick for any price and cost.

Contribution margin and fixed costs

Fixed costs do not change with each sale: salaries, tools, rent and software subscriptions. Variable costs grow with volume. The profit each additional sale contributes toward covering fixed costs is its contribution margin, which is price minus variable cost per unit.

Break-even point

Break-even volume = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin per unit.

Example: Fixed costs are $3,000 a month and each customer contributes $24 after variable costs. Break-even is 3,000 ÷ 24 = 125 customers. Below that you lose money each month, and above it every extra customer adds to profit.

Use this to set realistic targets and to test what happens if you change price or costs.

CAC and LTV

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is the total sales and marketing spend divided by the number of new customers it won in the same period.

Customer lifetime value (LTV) estimates the gross profit a customer generates before leaving. A simple version for subscriptions is:

LTV = Monthly revenue per customer × Gross margin ÷ Monthly churn rate.

Example: $30 revenue × 80% margin = $24 gross profit a month. If 4% of customers cancel each month, expected lifetime is about 25 months (1 ÷ 0.04), so LTV = 24 ÷ 0.04 = $600.

If CAC is $150, LTV is four times CAC. Many investors look for at least 3 to 1 as a rough rule of thumb, but the right ratio depends on your business and how fast you want to grow. Treat these as estimates, because churn changes as you learn.

CAC payback period

Payback period = CAC ÷ Monthly gross profit per customer. With a $150 CAC and $24 monthly gross profit, you recover acquisition costs in about 6 months (150 ÷ 24 = 6.25). Shorter payback means you can reinvest faster and need less cash to grow.

Profit is not cash

Profit is what remains after costs on paper. Cash is what is in the bank. A profitable business can run out of cash if customers pay late, you buy inventory in advance or you pay annual costs up front. Conversely, annual prepayments can make a young company cash-rich before it is profitable.

Burn rate is the net cash you spend per month, and runway is cash in the bank divided by monthly burn. With $18,000 in the bank and a net burn of $3,000 a month, you have six months. Revisit it monthly and decide early what to cut or fix, not in the last month.

The real cost of borrowing

If you finance equipment or growth with a loan, compare the total interest, not only the monthly payment. An EMI calculator shows the total repayable on a loan, and a compound interest calculator shows how savings or debt grow over time. Check whether projected cash flow can cover repayments even in a slow month.

A monthly numbers checklist

  • Revenue, COGS and gross margin.
  • New customers, CAC and payback period.
  • Churn rate and estimated LTV.
  • Fixed costs and break-even volume.
  • Cash in the bank, burn and runway.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good gross margin?

It varies by industry. Software businesses often have high gross margins, while physical products and services usually have lower ones. Compare against similar businesses.

Is an LTV to CAC ratio of 3 to 1 required?

It is a common rule of thumb, not a law. A lower ratio can work with fast payback or strong cash, and a higher one may mean you are under-investing in growth.

What is the difference between revenue and profit?

Revenue is total sales. Profit is what remains after subtracting costs. Gross profit subtracts direct costs, and net profit subtracts all costs.

How do I estimate churn for a new product?

Start with the share of customers who cancel each month once you have a few months of data. Early estimates are rough and should be updated as the data grows.
