Customer Discovery Interviews: Questions to Ask and Avoid

By Plert Ankush · Published Nov 12, 2026 · Updated Nov 12, 2026 · 3 min read

Customer interviews are the cheapest, fastest research a founder can do, and they are also easy to do badly. Ask "Would you buy this?" and almost everyone says yes. The answer feels encouraging and tells you almost nothing.

The aim of a discovery interview is to learn about the other person's life, problems and past behaviour, not to pitch. This guide shows how to find people, what to ask, what to avoid and how to turn conversations into decisions.

Key takeaways

  • Ask about specific past events, not opinions or future intentions.
  • Do not pitch your idea. Keep the conversation about their world.
  • Look for actions already taken: time, money and workarounds.
  • Write notes immediately and look for patterns across interviews.

Three principles

  1. Talk about their life, not your idea. The moment you describe your product, people try to be nice.
  2. Ask about the past, not the future. What someone did last week is reliable. What they say they would do next month is not.
  3. Look for commitment. Compliments are free. Time, money and introductions are not.

Rob Fitzpatrick's book The Mom Test popularised these rules and is worth reading in full. The summary here is our own.

Who to interview

Interview people who match the customer you wrote down, not whoever is easiest to reach. Good places to find them include:

  • online communities, forums and social groups where they discuss the problem;
  • colleagues of colleagues and warm introductions;
  • direct, short messages to people who publicly talk about the problem;
  • customers of competing or adjacent products.

Keep your request brief and honest: "I am researching how freelancers handle late invoices and would love 20 minutes of your experience. I am not selling anything."

A simple interview structure

  1. Warm up (2 minutes). Explain the purpose and ask about their role and context.
  2. Explore the problem (10 to 15 minutes). Ask them to walk through the last time it happened.
  3. Dig into what they do today (5 minutes). Tools, workarounds, costs and frustrations.
  4. Close (3 minutes). Ask who else you should talk to and whether you can follow up.

Good questions and bad questions

AvoidAsk instead
Would you use a tool that does X?Tell me about the last time you had to deal with X. What happened?
Do you think this is a good idea?How do you handle this today? What do you not like about it?
How much would you pay for this?What does this problem cost you in time or money each month?
Would you ever buy a product like this?Have you tried to fix this before? What did you try?
Don't you hate it when...?How do you feel about how that process works?

Useful follow-ups include "Why?", "What happened next?", "Can you show me?" and "Who else is involved?". Silence is also a tool. Let people finish and add more.

Signals worth trusting

  • They already pay or spend effort on a workaround, whether a tool, a freelancer or a spreadsheet they maintain.
  • They describe the problem emotionally, with real frustration and specific examples.
  • They ask when they can try it, offer to introduce others or agree to a follow-up.
  • They have looked for solutions and found the options lacking.

Signals to discount: "That sounds cool", "I would probably use that", vague enthusiasm and feature requests without a story behind them.

Ask for a small commitment

At the end, test how real the interest is with a proportionate request: a follow-up call, an introduction to a colleague, access to sample data, a pre-order or a deposit. If someone who loved the idea will not spend 15 more minutes or make one introduction, their enthusiasm was polite.

Take notes and find patterns

  1. Record the conversation if the person agrees, or take notes as you go, and write a summary within an hour.
  2. Separate facts (what they did) from opinions and from your interpretations.
  3. Keep a simple table: person, role, problem described, current solution, quotes, commitment.
  4. After ten or so interviews, tag recurring problems and group them. Count how many people mention each one.

Patterns across several people matter. A single vivid story does not make a market.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Pitching in the first five minutes.
  • Asking leading or hypothetical questions.
  • Talking more than the interviewee.
  • Only interviewing supportive friends.
  • Collecting compliments and counting them as evidence.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a discovery interview be?

Twenty to thirty minutes is usually enough. Respect the time you asked for.

Should I record the interview?

Recording helps you listen, but ask permission first and follow the rules where you and the participant are located. Otherwise take careful notes.

Should I pay people for their time?

Not required for short conversations with people who care about the problem, though a small thank-you can help when you ask busy professionals for longer sessions.

What if people do not have the problem?

That is useful information. Either you are talking to the wrong group, or the problem is not painful enough. Adjust your customer definition or choose another problem.
