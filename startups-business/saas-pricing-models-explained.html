SaaS Pricing Models Explained: Flat, Tiered, Per-Seat and Usage-Based

By Plert Ankush · Published Nov 12, 2026 · Updated Nov 12, 2026 · 3 min read

Pricing is one of the highest-leverage decisions in a software business, and one of the least tested. Founders often pick a number that "feels right", launch and never revisit it. Yet price shapes who signs up, how much you earn per customer and whether the business can survive.

This guide explains the main SaaS pricing models, how to choose what to charge for, how to structure plans and how to improve your pricing over time.

Key takeaways

  • Price on a value metric that grows as customers get more value.
  • Three tiers with a clear difference between them is a reliable starting structure.
  • Early prices are almost always too low. Test and raise them.
  • Pricing is an ongoing experiment, not a one-time decision.

Start with the value metric

A value metric is the unit you charge for, such as seats, projects, contacts, messages or gigabytes. A good one rises when the customer gets more value and is easy to understand and forecast.

  • An email tool charging per contact aligns with a customer's list growing.
  • A project management tool charging per user aligns with team size.
  • A storage product charging per gigabyte aligns with usage.

Ask: when a customer is delighted and uses more of our product, what grows? Charge for that.

Structuring tiers

A common pattern is three plans, often named something like Starter, Pro and Business.

  • Starter serves individuals or small teams and covers the core job.
  • Pro is the plan you want most customers to pick, with the features and limits a growing team needs.
  • Business adds advanced controls, higher limits and support for larger customers.

Make the differences meaningful and tied to the value metric. Do not hide the feature everyone needs in the highest tier, and do not create plans so similar that the choice is confusing. Show the plan you recommend visually.

How to set the price

  1. Work out your floor. Calculate your cost to serve a customer (hosting, support, payment fees) and the margin you need. See our guide to unit economics.
  2. Look at alternatives. What do customers pay now, including spreadsheets or manual work? Price in relation to the value and the options, not only to competitors.
  3. Ask customers. Questions such as "At what price would this be so expensive you would not consider it, and at what price would it be a bargain?" give a range, though real purchases are better evidence.
  4. Launch, observe and adjust. Watch conversion, churn and which plans people choose.

Monthly, annual and discounts

Offering annual billing at a discount, commonly the equivalent of one or two months free, improves cash flow and reduces churn because customers commit for longer. Calculate discounts carefully with a percentage calculator so you know the effective price. Be cautious with deep, permanent discounts that train customers to wait for sales.

When and how to raise prices

Early-stage prices are usually too low. Signs that you may be underpriced include almost no pushback, customers saying it is "a steal" and heavy demand you struggle to serve. Raising prices for new customers first is the lowest-risk approach. For existing customers, give notice, explain the added value and consider keeping loyal customers on their current price for a period.

Common pricing mistakes

  • Pricing by cost alone and ignoring the value delivered.
  • Copying a competitor without understanding their customers or costs.
  • Launching with many plans and add-ons.
  • Charging a metric customers cannot predict.
  • Never revisiting the price.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best pricing model for a new SaaS product?

There is no single best model. A simple tiered subscription priced on a clear value metric works for most early products. Use usage-based pricing when your costs and customer value scale directly with consumption.

Should I offer a free plan?

Only if the cost to serve free users is low and there is a clear path to upgrade. A free trial is often a safer start for products that need setup.

How many pricing tiers should I have?

Three is a common default because it makes comparison easy. Start simple and add complexity only when customers ask for it.

How often should I change prices?

Review pricing regularly, for example once or twice a year, and after major product improvements.
